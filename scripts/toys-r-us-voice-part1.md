# Toys R Us — Voice Script PART 1 of 2
**Characters (with spaces): 6,666 | Words: 1,151**
**Covers: Hook → Chapter 1: The Rise → Chapter 2: The Deal**

---

It is March 15, 2018. A press release lands at 7 a.m. It is two paragraphs long. It says that Toys R Us — all 735 of its remaining American stores — will close. Every single one. The shelves will be emptied. The leases will be surrendered. The registers will go dark. Thirty-three thousand people will lose their jobs.

Nobody in those stores got a phone call. Nobody got pulled into a meeting. They found out the same way everyone else did — online, on their phones, in the break room, staring at a screen. And by the way: their severance package was zero dollars.

Seven days later, Charles Lazarus — the 94-year-old man who had built this company from nothing, who had given his entire life to it — died. Whether the news broke him, nobody can say for certain. But the timing is not something you can easily look away from.

So how does the most powerful toy company in the history of the world end up here? And who exactly made that happen?

The year is 1948, and a 25-year-old Army veteran named Charles Lazarus has an idea. He opens a store called Children's Bargain Town in Washington D.C., selling baby furniture. He is not yet thinking about toys. He is thinking about young couples coming home from the war, starting families, needing things. He is thinking about where the money is going.

Within a decade, Lazarus notices something. The furniture customers come once. The toy customers come back every Christmas, every birthday, every time a child outgrows something or breaks something or simply wants something new. In 1957, he converts the store entirely to toys. He is not selling products. He is selling an occasion that repeats forever.

By 1966, Lazarus has expanded enough that he sells the company to Interstate Stores, a department store chain, for $7.5 million. He stays on as CEO. It is a shrewd arrangement — he takes the cash, keeps the control. Then, in 1974, Interstate Stores files for bankruptcy. Everything they own is worthless, except one thing: Toys R Us is profitable. In the middle of a corporate collapse, Lazarus's creation is the only lifeboat. He buys it back. He takes it public in 1978 on the New York Stock Exchange, and this time it belongs entirely to itself.

What follows is a sustained period of dominance so complete it borders on the monopolistic. By 1985, Toys R Us controls 20 percent of all toy sales in the United States. The entire toy industry revolves around its purchasing decisions. If Mattel or Hasbro wanted their product on the most important shelf in America, they had to deal with Toys R Us. Full stop.

In the 1990s, the company goes global. Japan, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia. By the middle of the decade, revenue hits $11 billion. Eleven billion dollars — selling toys. Lazarus has created something that did not exist before him: the category killer, the warehouse-style mega-store that so thoroughly outprices and out-selections every competitor that competition itself becomes beside the point.

And then, in November 2001, Toys R Us opens the most extravagant retail space in America. The Times Square flagship. One hundred and ten thousand square feet. A sixty-foot working Ferris wheel inside the building. A life-size animatronic Tyrannosaurus rex. A three-story Barbie Dream House. It is not a store. It is a monument to the belief that this company will last forever.

At the moment that monument was being bolted together, the decisions that would destroy it were already being made.

The first crack appears in 1996. That is the year Walmart — not a toy store, not a specialty retailer, just a place that sells everything — surpasses Toys R Us as the number one toy seller in the United States. Toys R Us had been untouchable for twenty years. Now it is number two. The logic is simple and brutal: Walmart can sell toys at a loss and make the money back on groceries, electronics, and clothing. Toys R Us cannot. It only sells toys.

The company's leadership responds to this pressure by doing something that, in hindsight, is the most consequential mistake in retail history. In the year 2000, Toys R Us CEO John Eyler signs a deal with a company called Amazon. Amazon is still young. It is still primarily a bookseller. The deal gives Amazon approximately $50 million in exchange for a guarantee: Toys R Us will be the exclusive toy seller on Amazon's platform for two years. Eyler's logic is sound — why build your own expensive e-commerce infrastructure when you can rent someone else's?

Within a year, Amazon violates the agreement. It begins allowing third-party sellers to list toys alongside Toys R Us on the same page. Competitors appear. Prices undercut. The exclusivity deal — the very reason Toys R Us had handed over $50 million and stopped building its own platform — is quietly, methodically, rendered meaningless. Toys R Us sues Amazon in 2004. A New Jersey court eventually rules in their favor in 2006. By then, it does not matter. Toys R Us has spent four critical years standing still while the digital economy rewired itself around them. They have no platform. They have no infrastructure. They have nothing.

Into this moment of weakness, in 2005, walk three firms: Kohlberg Kravis Roberts — KKR — co-founded by Henry Kravis, the man who had essentially invented the modern leveraged buyout with the 1989 takeover of RJR Nabisco. Bain Capital, the Boston private equity firm. And Vornado Realty Trust, whose interest is transparently in the real estate — 1,500 locations on prime retail land around the world. Together, they offer $6.6 billion to take Toys R Us private.

Here is how leveraged buyouts work. The buyers put in $1.3 billion of their own money. They borrow the remaining $5.3 billion. And then — this is the critical part — they attach that debt to the company they just bought. Toys R Us did not take out those loans. KKR, Bain, and Vornado did. But Toys R Us is now legally responsible for repaying them. The $400 million in annual interest payments does not come from the private equity firms' bank accounts. It comes from the toy stores. From every Geoffrey the Giraffe stuffed animal sold. From every bicycle assembled in a back room by a worker making $11 an hour.

The deal closes in July 2005. Toys R Us is profitable at the time of purchase. Let that sentence sit for a moment. This is not a turnaround story. This is not three firms saving a failing company. They are buying a functioning, revenue-generating business — and immediately attaching a $5.3 billion anchor to its ankle. Whether the company can swim fast enough to stay alive is, frankly, not their primary concern.

Their primary concern is the return.
