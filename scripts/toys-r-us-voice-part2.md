Covers: The Spiral through Outro


KKR, Bain, and Vornado now own Toys R Us. They have attached 5.3 billion dollars in debt to a company that was profitable when they bought it. The interest alone will cost 400 million dollars every single year. What happens next is not complicated. It is just slow.

For the first few years, the numbers hold together, barely. Toys R Us is generating revenue. People are still buying toys in physical stores. The 400 million dollar annual interest payment is brutal, but it is manageable. And so the private equity owners make the rational calculation of people who will not be around to see the consequences: they do not invest in the company. They extract from it.

Between 2005 and the eventual bankruptcy, KKR, Bain, and Vornado collect approximately 470 million dollars in fees, management charges, and dividends from Toys R Us. That 470 million dollars does not go into new stores. It does not go into a website. It does not go into employee training or competitive pricing or the technology investments that Amazon, Target, and Walmart are pouring billions into. It goes out the door, into private accounts, into quarterly reports that make the partners look very good.

Meanwhile, the world is changing faster than any debt laden company can respond. Amazon is no longer a bookseller. By 2008, it is a platform for everything. By 2010, its toy selection is larger than any physical store could ever be. By 2012, same day delivery is beginning in major cities. The fundamental value proposition of Toys R Us, selection and expertise, is being replicated digitally, at lower prices, with the added convenience that customers never have to leave their homes.

CEO Gerald Storch, who runs the company from 2006 to 2013, is not an incompetent man. He sees what is happening. But seeing a problem and solving a problem are different things when your company is paying 400 million dollars per year in interest before it spends a single dollar on anything else. Every proposal to build a serious ecommerce platform runs into the same wall: there is no money. There has never been money, not since July 2005, because the money is paying the interest on the debt that the company did not choose to take on.

The warning signs accumulate year by year. Store traffic begins declining in 2010. The in store experience, already outdated, falls further behind as competitors renovate. Target redesigns its toy departments. Walmart expands its online fulfillment. Apple opens the App Store in 2008, and within five years, digital gaming for children explodes. Every trend is moving against physical toy retail, and Toys R Us cannot pivot because it cannot invest because it cannot breathe under the debt.

In 2015, the Times Square flagship, that monument to permanence, that sixty foot Ferris wheel, that declaration that Toys R Us was not going anywhere, closes. It is the symbolic collapse before the financial one. The most famous toy store in America, on the most famous street in America, shuts down because the rent is 20 million dollars per year and the company cannot justify it. The Ferris wheel is dismantled. The T Rex is removed. By the time they finish clearing it out, it looks like everything that came after.

By the summer of 2017, the people who supply Toys R Us are nervous. Moodys has the companys debt rated Caa2, deep junk territory, two notches above default. Suppliers, Mattel, Hasbro, LEGO, are watching the ratings. They are thinking about the upcoming holiday season. They are thinking about what happens if they ship 200 million dollars in product and the company files for bankruptcy before it pays them.

On September 18, 2017, Toys R Us files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. The stated debt load is 5 billion dollars. The stated reason, in the companys own filing, is the need to restructure that debt and invest in digital capabilities, the digital capabilities they could not invest in for twelve years because they were paying off debt. It is a filing that manages to be both legally precise and deeply ironic.

The filing is supposed to be a reorganization, not a death sentence. Dave Brandon, the CEO brought in from Dominos Pizza in 2015 to fix things, assures employees and the public that this is a controlled process. The stores will remain open for the holidays. No one needs to panic.

But the suppliers do not believe him. The moment the Chapter 11 filing hits the wires, Mattel, Hasbro, and dozens of smaller manufacturers begin restricting credit. They demand cash upfront for holiday shipments or they do not ship at all. The holiday season, the single season that generates the majority of annual toy revenue, arrives in a company that cannot fully stock its shelves because its suppliers no longer trust it to pay them. The companys last chance to generate the cash needed to reorganize evaporates in the toy aisle.

January 2018: 182 store closures are announced. Then, on March 15, 2018, at 7 a.m., the press release lands. All remaining US stores. Full liquidation. Everything must go. Thirty three thousand American employees receive no advance notice. They learn from the news. When they ask about severance, the answer is: there is no money. The answer is zero.

On March 22, 2018, Charles Lazarus dies in his sleep at age 94. In the final week of his life, he had watched every newspaper and television channel tell him what would happen to the company he had built before he had any money, in the years after the war, when the only thing he had was an idea about what young families would need. Seven days. He outlived his company by seven days.

After the liquidation is announced, workers begin organizing. They show up outside the New York offices of KKR. They show up outside Bain Capitals office in Boston. They are wearing red polo shirts, the Toys R Us employee uniform, and they are holding signs asking for severance pay. They demand a 75 million dollar severance fund.

Congressional hearings follow. Senators ask KKR, Bain, and Vornado to explain how 470 million dollars in fees and dividends were extracted from a company that then left 33,000 workers with nothing. The executives who answer do so in the careful language of legal compliance. They did nothing wrong. The debt structure was standard. The market changed. These things happen in retail.

The three firms eventually contribute 20 million dollars to a severance fund. Workers had asked for 75 million dollars. They received 20 million dollars. Dave Brandon, the CEO who presided over the collapse, received retention bonuses during the bankruptcy proceedings. The three private equity firms faced no legal penalties.

KKRs fund that participated in the Toys R Us deal generated returns. Not from Toys R Us itself, the equity was wiped out, but the fees collected over twelve years added up. The three firms are still in business. They are still doing deals. The model that killed Toys R Us is not in any way illegal, and it has not stopped.

Toys R Us is not a story about toys. It is a story about a financial instrument that allows you to buy something, load it with debt, collect your fees, and walk away before the bill comes due. Charles Lazarus built that company in seventy years. KKR, Bain, and Vornado destroyed it in twelve, and made money doing it. They have done it to dozens of companies since. One of them is the story of how an entire American industry disappeared in a single decade.

That story is next.
